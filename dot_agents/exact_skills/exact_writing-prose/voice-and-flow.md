# Voice and Flow

Sentence-level rhythm, structure, and rhetorical patterns. For the hard-no list of LLM tells (binary contrasts, staccato triples, intensifiers, jargon, meta-voice), see [anti-patterns](anti-patterns.md) first — this file is the depth reference for what doesn't fit there.

## Cliche Relational Phrases

Trying to build casual intimacy with the reader — "this isn't like those stuffy business articles, I'm a cool writer" — via sassy cliches or slang is never effective.

- "Before you know it, X creeps in"
- "You know how it is, ..."
- "X is true, I promise"
- "We're all X these days, ..."
- "We're all guilty of X, but"

### "Real Talk" Rhythm and Tone

A subset of the above: the sarcastic, overly direct "no-nonsense" truth-teller tone.

Common tells:

- "Look," used as a setup for "real talk"
- Rhetorical questions with immediate answers: "Is that what you want? Of course not."
- Setups like "Most people..., but the truth is..." that put assumptions on the reader just to refute them

## Meta-Commentary

Self-referential asides:

- "Hint:"
- "Plot twist:" / "Spoiler:"
- "You already know this, but"
- "But that's another post"
- "What I'm trying to say here is"

## Telling the Reader What They Think or Feel

Don't project mental states onto the reader:

- "You probably thought that"
- "Now I know what you're thinking, but"
- "Most people think you should X, but"
- "It feels like I'm saying X, but I'm not"

For the related pattern of restating context the reader already has, see [anti-patterns](anti-patterns.md).

## Rhythm

### Staccato Paragraphs

Avoid paragraphs of *only* short punchy sentences:

> Ruby is a dynamically typed language. It is foundationally object-oriented. That is, everything is an object. It also has a powerful standard library. Ruby on Rails made it extremely popular for web development. Matz designed Ruby to be a joyful language to write. It has a passionate community.

Direct, punchy writing is good — but balance it with longer sentences and varied rhythm. Sentences that all hit with the same length and intensity read like a list when read aloud.

### Asides within Asides

The opposite problem: paragraph-sized sentences that become a maze of metaphors:

> a movement for social justice, which starts from a fertile vision of the world in which no one is diminished yet falters in its attempts to arrive at such an arrangement by means of calamitous shortcuts -ultimately distorting the centuries-long arc of moral progress as it advances. In ways both obvious and subtle, it is in that warp that fresh injustices fester.

Readers' vision fuzzes out. Even when individually constructed well, stacking too many ideas in one sentence breaks the flow.

### Subject Last

A specific passive-voice trap that lures you in but never works. It's tempting to open by describing something, then realize you need to qualify it with an aside, so you slap in em dashes. It sounds interesting while writing; a simple example exposes the flaw:

- Bad: "One type of dog that is cute — and possessed of a friendly disposition — is the Australian Shepherd."
- Good: "Australian Shepherds are cute and have a friendly disposition."

Real example:

> One aspect of centralization with a bad rap — the thing people most point to when extolling the virtues of decentralization — is _process_.

Better:

> The marquee issue that proponents of decentralization claim to be eliminating is _process_.

## Rhetorical Setups

These announce insight rather than deliver it:

| Pattern | Problem |
| --- | --- |
| "What if [reframe]?" | Socratic posturing |
| "Here's what I mean:" | Redundant preview |
| "Think about it:" | Condescending prompt |
| "And that's okay." | Unnecessary permission |

Make the point. Let readers draw conclusions.

## Formulaic Constructions

| Pattern | Problem |
| --- | --- |
| "By the time X, I was Y." | Narrative template |
| "X that isn't Y" | Indirect. Say "X is broken" |

## Em Dashes and Asides

Em dashes are fun and asides are useful, but overuse destroys flow. If you can split a sentence into multiple sentences, do it. Preserve asides only when they *improve* the flow when read aloud. In a patch of short, staccato sentences, a longer sentence with a well-placed aside can add variety.

When an aside is the right move, consider commas, em dashes, or a parenthetical:

- **Commas** — short asides tightly connected to the main sentence
- **Em dashes** — asides that need longer pauses and more emphasis, or contain a list
- **Parentheticals** — examples or explanations that add helpful context but sit tangential to the sentence

**Good asides:**

- "The website is built with Next.js (a popular JavaScript framework for building web apps) and services millions of users per day."
- "The experience of climbing the mountain, while one of the most challenging things I've ever done, changed my life in ways I could never have achieved otherwise."
- "It's my favorite book — the story, the characters, the magic system — every aspect is deeply immersive and original."

The em-dash *zinger payoff* — em-dashes setting up an aphoristic closer — is a separate problem; see [anti-patterns](anti-patterns.md).

## Passive Voice

Try rewriting passive sentences as active — it often shortens and sharpens. But if the active version feels unnatural or awkward, keep the passive. The same applies to ending a sentence with a preposition or starting with "And" or "But." Try the rewrite, but don't twist prose for the sake of dogma.

| Pattern | Problem |
| --- | --- |
| "The engineers were overworked by management, and became burnt out." | Describing events passively |
| "Mistakes were made." | Avoiding responsibility with the passive voice |

Better:

- "Management overworked the engineers, burning them out."
- "We made mistakes."

## Purple Prose

Poetic, adjective-rich, heavily metaphoric writing that reveals the writer's ego to the reader. In trying to impress, the writer alienates. Look for maze-like sentence structures and multiple sentences stuffed with adjectives.

If a passage is going purple, interrogate whether each metaphor adds value. If so, carve it to the essence of what's helpful and keep only that.

## Good Voice and Flow

What strong prose looks like in the positive:

- Clear and direct, active, taking responsibility
- Topic-focused — not the writer, the reader, or the relationship between them
- Vivid verb choices instead of bland verbs propped up by adverbs
- Original metaphors when they illuminate from a useful angle
- Adjectives that are evocative and precise, splashed in carefully (not stacked into purple prose)
- Minimal jargon, especially non-technical business cliches
- Varied rhythm that builds and resolves tension
- Satisfying flow when read aloud
